12.1The build arc — meet Forge

For eleven lectures, "the robot" has meant a professional, pre-engineered platform: the AgileX LIMO, with its four bolt-on drive modules, its sealed motor drivers, its factory-calibrated sensors. You learned to drive it, simulate it, sense with it, localize on it, and — in Lecture 11 — open it up and understand the real electronics inside it well enough to test and trust it.

For these last three lectures, you build your own. Not in simulation — a real, physical, wheeled robot, assembled from a bare gear motor, an empty microSD card, and a spool of wire, using every single skill this course has given you: H-bridges and PWM from Lecture 11, ROS 2 node/topic architecture from Lecture 2, URDF from Lecture 4, sensor drivers from Lecture 7, Gazebo from Lecture 6. Nothing here is new in kind — it's everything you already know, applied without a manufacturer's chassis doing the hard parts for you.

Say hello to Forge — the name we'll use throughout this build arc for the differential-drive robot you're about to wire, program, design, and bring to life.

📖 Forge is a build of the FastBot reference design

Lectures 12-14 follow the FastBot reference design from The Construct's course Build Your First ROS 2 Based Robot. Everything here — part choices, pin assignments, firmware, the ROS 2 driver, the CAD documents, the Gazebo plugins — comes from that build, so every command and config file in these three sessions is copy-paste-correct against real, published hardware.

One consequence worth flagging up front: the ROS 2 package names stay fastbot_* (fastbot_description, fastbot_bringup, fastbot_gazebo), the default robot_name stays fastbot, and the Pi's hostname is fastbot. That is deliberate, not a typo — it keeps your workspace aligned with the upstream CAD documents and reference repositories. "Forge" is what you call the machine on your bench; fastbot is what its software calls itself. If you'd rather rename everything, do it after Lecture 14, when nothing external depends on the names any more.

12.3Motors — specifications and pinout

Choosing the motors is the single most consequential decision about how Forge interacts with the physical world. Forge is small and light, so almost any hobby gear motor could physically move it — which means the choice comes down to two things that have nothing to do with raw torque.

Decision 1: voltage

Whatever voltage the motors need, the battery has to supply — and the same battery also has to feed the Raspberry Pi, the LiDAR, and the camera. Motors rated for 12 V hit the sweet spot: they're the most widely available class, they typically run happily anywhere in a 5-12 V range, and 12 V sits comfortably within reach of a 3S LiPo (11.1 V nominal, ~12.6 V charged) that can also feed a 5 V rail for everything else. §12.8-12.10 close that loop.

Decision 2: encoders

An encoder tracks how far the motor shaft has actually turned. Without one you have an open-loop system: you command "half power" and hope. With one you have a closed-loop system: the controller reads back real rotation and corrects the command until the wheel is genuinely doing what you asked. The practical difference is visible — closed-loop motion is smoother, holds speed under load, and drives straighter, because the two wheels no longer drift apart just because one motor happens to be slightly stronger than the other.

🌍 Encoders are also non-negotiable for odometry

Beyond smooth motion, the encoder counts are what Forge's driver node turns into /odom in Lecture 13 — the same wheel odometry you fused with an IMU through an EKF back in Lecture 9, and the same signal AMCL and Nav2 lean on in Lecture 10. A robot without encoders cannot do dead reckoning at all. And since a motor with an integrated encoder costs barely more than one without, there is no reason to compromise here.

Those two constraints land on a very common, very cheap part: the DC gear reduced motor with encoder.

⚠️ Never trust a wire colour from memory

Wire colours on these motors are not standardised across vendors, and frequently differ between batches of the same model from the same supplier. A red wire on one motor might be encoder power; on the next unit out of the same bag, red might be a motor terminal carrying real current straight into a signal pin that was never designed for it. This is the single most common way to release the magic smoke from a fresh motor or Nano. Every time you wire a motor — even one that looks identical to yesterday's — check the pinout published by the seller you bought it from, and confirm it against Figure 12.7.

12.4Motor control hardware — L298N & Arduino Nano

A motor connected straight to a battery spins one way, at one speed, forever. To make it "smart" — fast, slow, forward, backward, on demand — two pieces of hardware sit between the battery and the motor.

The L298N: direction and current

A DC motor reverses when you reverse the polarity across its terminals. Doing that under software control means an H-bridge: four switches arranged so that closing one diagonal pair drives the motor one way and closing the other pair drives it the other. You met this concept on LIMO in Lecture 11, sealed inside the chassis. On Forge it's a board on your bench: the L298N.

The L298N does three jobs at once:

  • Direction — bidirectional control by switching DC polarity.
  • Current amplification — an Arduino pin can source milliamps; motors want amps. The L298N stands between them.
  • Speed — it accepts PWM (pulse-width modulation) on its control inputs.
📖 PWM in one paragraph

PWM controls power by switching a signal on and off very rapidly and varying the fraction of time it's on — the duty cycle. 100% duty cycle means the signal is always on, so full power. 50% means it's on half the time, so roughly half power. 0% means always off, so no power. The motor's own inertia smooths the chopped supply into steady rotation. Below some low duty cycle — the motor's deadband — you get audible buzzing and no visible motion at all, exactly the symptom Lecture 11 warned you to expect on real hardware.

The Arduino Nano: generating that PWM

Something has to produce the PWM signal. It could in principle be the Raspberry Pi — but it shouldn't be. Forge uses a small Arduino Nano (or any compatible ATmega328P board) dedicated to the motor loop.

Why a dedicated MCU?What it means in practice
Stable, reliable PWM generationThe Nano has dedicated hardware PWM timers, so the signal stays smooth and consistent. On a Linux PC, PWM is software-scheduled — a heavy computational load can visibly degrade the signal.
Real-time performanceThe Nano runs one loop and nothing else, so motors respond immediately. A general-purpose OS is not real-time; other processes introduce delays and jitter.
Offloading the main computerThe Pi should spend its cycles on ROS 2 nodes, perception and autonomous navigation — not on bit-banging a motor signal. It just tells the Nano what speed it wants.
🌍 You have seen this architecture before — on LIMO

This is the exact same split AgileX designed into LIMO and you studied in Lecture 11: a general-purpose Linux computer running ROS 2, and a separate embedded controller that owns motor PWM and encoder reading, talking to each other over a serial link. Forge's Nano is that lesson applied by your own hand. A heavy perception callback on the Pi must never be able to delay a motor command long enough for Forge to behave unpredictably. Split "thinking" from "moving," and you'll recognise the pattern on almost every serious wheeled robot you meet after this course.

12.5Wiring Forge, step by step

Time to connect things. Work through this in order, and keep the wheels off the ground (or the motors unmounted) until §12.7 — a mis-wired first test that sends the robot off the bench is a very avoidable way to break a LiDAR.

The direction logic is simple, and identical for both channels:

IN1 = HIGH, IN2 = LOW   ->  Motor A forward
IN1 = LOW,  IN2 = HIGH  ->  Motor A backward
IN1 = LOW,  IN2 = LOW   ->  Motor A stopped

(IN3 / IN4 behave identically for Motor B)
🔧 If ENA/ENB are jumpered HIGH, where does speed control come from?

Reasonable question — many L298N tutorials put PWM on ENA/ENB and use IN1-IN4 as pure digital direction pins. Forge does it the other way round. The four Nano pins chosen below (D5, D6, D9, D10) are all hardware-PWM-capable pins on the ATmega328P, and the ros_arduino_bridge firmware you'll flash in §12.6 drives them with PWM: to go forward at 60% it holds one input at 0 and PWMs the other at 60% duty. Enable stays permanently high, so the jumpers can stay where the board shipped them. Two valid designs; the firmware decides which one you're using, which is exactly why the pin assignments below are not arbitrary.

Step 1 — Motors to the L298N

Connect each motor's power pair (wires 05 and 06, the red 🔴 and black ⚫ pair on most units — confirm against your pinout) to the OUT terminals. Because one motor is physically mirrored relative to the other, its two wires go on flipped compared to the first.

💡 Getting this backwards costs you nothing

If you flip a motor pair the wrong way, the worst case is that Forge drives backwards when you tell it to go forwards during the §12.7 bench test. Swap that motor's two OUT wires and move on. This is genuinely a 30-second fix, and it is a much better failure mode than the alternatives elsewhere in this section.

Step 2 — L298N control inputs to the Nano

The IN pins go to digital GPIO pins on the Nano. The specific pin numbers are not arbitrary — they must match what the firmware in §12.6 expects:

IN3D9Right motor, one direction (PWM-capable)
IN4D5Right motor, other direction (PWM-capable)

Now close the feedback loop. Each motor's two encoder signal wires (02 and 03) go to the Nano:

The encoders also need power. For this bench test, take 5 V and GND from the Nano's own pins (wires 04 and 01). Later, once the power module is in place (§12.10), you'll move that 5 V feed to the robot's regulated rail instead.

Step 4 — Battery to the L298N

For the very first test the LiPo connects directly to the L298N's +12V / GND terminals, through a T-connector pigtail whose bare ends you can screw into the terminal block. (§12.10 inserts the power module between them permanently — this direct connection is a temporary, motors-only test rig.)

⚠️ Check polarity twice before you connect once

This is the moment where a mistake is expensive. Red is positive, black is ground, and a reversed connector on a freshly wired robot is a fast way to destroy an L298N, a Nano, or eventually the Pi. Unlike the flipped motor wires in Step 1, this one does not politely fail — it releases smoke. Meter it if you have any doubt.

Step 5 — Everything together

12.6Firmware — ros_arduino_bridge on the Nano

The Nano is wired but empty. It needs firmware that (a) reads the encoders, (b) closes a velocity loop, and (c) accepts commands over serial. Rather than writing that from scratch, Forge uses a well-established open-source package: Josh Newans' fork of ros_arduino_bridge. It converts velocity commands arriving over UART into PWM on the L298N's inputs, and reports encoder counts back.

The Nano connects to your development PC with a USB-to-USB-mini cable. Do this from your own PC, not from the Pi — you'll want the Arduino IDE's GUI.

Get the firmware

$ cd $ mkdir git-repo $ cd git-repo $ git clone https://github.com/joshnewans/ros_arduino_bridge.git

Install the Arduino IDE

$ sudo apt update $ sudo apt install arduino

On Windows or macOS, install the IDE from arduino.cc/en/software instead.

Upload

  1. Open the Arduino IDE, then File → Open and select ~/git-repo/ros_arduino_bridge/ROSArduinoBridge/ROSArduinoBridge.ino.
  2. Select the board: Tools → Board → Arduino Nano.
  3. Select the port: Tools → Port — usually /dev/ttyUSB0 or similar.
  4. Click Upload and watch for a successful compile-and-flash message.
⚠️ "Processor: ATmega328P (Old Bootloader)"

A very common snag with clone Nanos: the upload fails with a timeout or avrdude: stk500_recv() errors even though the board and port are right. Most inexpensive Nano clones ship with the old bootloader. Go to Tools → Processor and switch to ATmega328P (Old Bootloader), then upload again. This is not in the reference material and it will cost you twenty minutes if nobody tells you.

✅ With a successful upload, the Nano's firmware is live. Time to make something move.

12.7First motion & encoder calibration

This is the payoff for §12.3-12.6. Before you start: make sure the battery is powering the motors through the L298N, the Nano is connected to your PC by USB, and the wheels are off the ground.

A serial terminal

You need a tool to talk to the Nano over serial. The reference build uses SerialTool (Windows, macOS, Linux); the Arduino IDE's built-in Serial Monitor works too, as does screen /dev/ttyUSB0 57600 if you prefer a terminal.

🔩 57600 is not a suggestion

The baud rate must match the firmware exactly. A mismatch does not produce an error — it produces garbage bytes, silently. Remember this number: it reappears as the baud_rate parameter of the ROS 2 driver node in Lecture 13, and it must match there too.

The command set

Test 1 — open loop

> o 255 255 OK # both wheels spin at full power > o 0 0 OK

🎉 Your robot's wheels just moved under your own wiring and your own firmware. If one wheel turns the wrong way, that's the Step-1 fix from §12.5 — swap that motor's two OUT wires.

Test 2 — do the encoders work?

> r OK > e 0 0 # now turn each wheel by hand, then: > e 3450 -3450

Non-zero, changing numbers mean the hall-effect encoders are being read correctly. A channel stuck at zero means a wiring fault on that motor's A/B lines or its 5 V/GND feed. Fix that before continuing — closed-loop control without encoders is worse than open loop, not better.

Test 3 — closed loop

> m -255 -255 OK # motors spin the opposite way, and you can hear the controller # actively adjusting to hold the commanded rate

Calibrating counts per revolution

The controller needs to know how many encoder counts correspond to one full motor revolution. This value — counts per revolution (encoder_cpr) — is a property of your specific encoder-and-gearbox combination, so you have to measure it:

  1. Reset the encoder: r
  2. Turn the wheel by hand through exactly 1 revolution.
  3. Read the encoder: e. That number is one estimate of counts/rev.
  4. Reset again, turn 5 revolutions, read, and divide by 5.
  5. Reset again, turn 10 revolutions, read, and divide by 10.
  6. Average the three estimates. That's your encoder_cpr.

For the reference build this lands around 3450 counts/rev. Yours will differ — a different gear ratio changes it completely.

📖 From counts/rev to counts/loop

The m command doesn't take counts per revolution — it takes counts per PID loop. ros_arduino_bridge runs its PID loop at 30 Hz, so converting is one division: 

counts_per_loop = counts_per_revolution / PID_loop_rate

              = 3450 counts/rev / 30 loops/sec
              = 115 counts/loop     (for a target of 1 rev/sec)

So each PID cycle should accumulate 115 counts if the wheel is turning once per second. That same conversion, generalised, is exactly what the ROS 2 driver node computes in Lecture 13 — you're deriving it by hand now so the code isn't magic later.

> m 115 115 OK # each wheel should now hold very close to 1 revolution per second

Verify it with a stopwatch and a mark on the tyre: ten revolutions should take about ten seconds. If it doesn't, your measured counts/rev is wrong — repeat the calibration.

⚠️ A deliberate inconsistency to notice

In Lecture 13 you'll meet two reference files that declare encoder_cpr defaults of 2500 (the driver launch file) and 2550 (the bringup launch file) — neither of which is the 3450 derived above. That's not a trick question, it's what real projects look like: a default value gets typed once, copied into a second file, and drifts. Your measured value is the only one that's true for your robot. Set it deliberately in both places, and treat any hardcoded default you didn't measure yourself as a placeholder.

12.8The power budget

The motors run. Now they need to run off a battery you chose on purpose. Every component on Forge draws current at some voltage, and every watt has to come from a pack with finite stored energy. Before choosing a battery you need an honest power budget — not a guess.

The core formula:

P (watts) = V (volts) × I (amps)

Applied component by component:

ComponentVoltage (V)Current draw (A)Power (W) = V × A
Raspberry Pi 452.512.5
Motor driver (L298N)12224
Motors (2×)12336
Camera50.52.5
LiDAR50.73.5
Total—8.778.5 W
📖 This is a worst case, on purpose

This table assumes every component draws its rated current simultaneously and continuously — motors at full sustained torque, everything running at once. Real usage is usually kinder, which is exactly why a power budget is a design ceiling to size against, not a prediction of typical use. Budget for the worst case and get a pleasant surprise; budget for the average and watch a demo die in front of an audience.

From total power draw you can estimate runtime, once you know a pack's energy capacity:

Energy (Wh)  = Voltage (V) × Capacity (Ah)
Runtime (h)  = Energy (Wh) / Power (W)

Worked through for the 11.1 V, 2200 mAh (2.2 Ah) pack from §12.9:

Energy  = 11.1 V × 2.2 Ah  = 24.42 Wh
Runtime = 24.42 Wh / 78.5 W  ≈ 0.31 h  ≈ 18-22 minutes

Eighteen to twenty-two minutes of guaranteed worst-case runtime is short — and that is exactly the point of doing this arithmetic before buying a battery rather than after.

🤔 Think About It

You just budgeted assuming every component draws its rated current all the time. In reality, does a motor draw the same current sitting still under load, accelerating from rest, and cruising at constant speed? Which of those three is the number in the table closest to — and what does that mean for how much headroom this budget really has?

12.9Battery selection & safety

With the power requirements known, you can pick a pack. Forge uses a LiPo (lithium-polymer) — a rechargeable chemistry with high energy density that can also deliver high discharge currents. Unlike traditional lithium-ion cells, LiPos use a soft polymer electrolyte, which allows flexible, compact shapes. They're the standard choice in robotics, drones and RC because they hold voltage well under load and handle high power demands efficiently.

SpecForge's packWhat it tells you
Nominal voltage11.1 V (3S)Three cells in series at 3.7 V each; ~12.6 V fully charged. Suits 12 V motors, and regulates down cleanly to 5 V for the Pi.
Capacity2200 mAh (2.2 Ah)Total stored charge; feeds straight into the runtime formula in §12.8.
Discharge rating35 CMaximum safe continuous current = Capacity(Ah) × C = 2.2 × 35 = 77 A.
ConnectorXT60 / T-connectorMatch the gender and type to your harness before you order.

Forge's total draw is about 8.7 A (from the budget table). Against a 77 A continuous rating, the pack is nowhere near being the bottleneck — which is exactly the confirmation you want. The failure mode to avoid is the opposite: a pack with plenty of energy capacity but too low a C-rating for your peak current. Under load it sags badly or trips its own protection, which is the same voltage-sag warning sign Lecture 11 taught you to watch for in LIMO's battery_voltage field.

⚠️ Lithium safety, restated for a robot you wired yourself

Everything Lecture 11 taught about lithium handling on LIMO applies without exception here: never leave a pack fully discharged for long, always charge with a proper balance charger in a fire-safe bag or case on a non-flammable surface, never puncture or short the terminals, and retire any pack showing swelling, unusual heat or casing damage. A DIY build adds a risk LIMO's sealed pack doesn't have — you wired the connector. Double-check polarity before every first connection of a new harness, and never over-discharge: below roughly 3.0 V per cell you are permanently damaging the pack.

12.10Power distribution — the DFRobot module

One battery, several very different consumers. The motors want ~12 V and are electrically noisy. The Raspberry Pi wants a clean, well-regulated 5 V and will misbehave — or corrupt its SD card — if that rail sags. The LiDAR, camera and Nano also live on 5 V. You need something between the pack and the robot that transforms and distributes power.

Forge uses the DFRobot Power Module. It takes the raw LiPo input and provides both a switched 12 V output for the motor driver and a regulated 5 V output for everything else, with a physical power switch — which is more valuable than it sounds when you want to kill a runaway robot in a hurry.

Remember the direct battery-to-L298N connection from §12.5, Step 4? Now the module goes between them:

flowchart LR BATT["3S LiPo
11.1 V, 2200 mAh"] --> PM["DFRobot
Power Module"] PM -- "12 V rail" --> L298["L298N"] L298 --> ML["Left motor"] L298 --> MR["Right motor"] PM -- "regulated 5 V rail" --> PI["Raspberry Pi 4"] PM -- "5 V" --> ENC["Motor encoders"] PI -- "USB" --> LID["LiDAR"] PI -- "CSI ribbon" --> CAM["Camera"] PI -- "USB serial" --> NANO["Arduino Nano"] NANO --> L298 style BATT fill:#fdf3e4,stroke:#c8862c style PM fill:#fdeee4,stroke:#c8622c style PI fill:#eef0ff,stroke:#3d4bf5 style NANO fill:#f4effe,stroke:#8b5cf6
🔧 Why motor power and logic power stay separate

Look at the diagram again: the module feeds two separate rails. That separation is not cosmetic. Starting, stopping and reversing a motor induces voltage spikes on its supply line. If that same rail powered the Pi's or the Nano's logic, those spikes could reset or corrupt them mid-operation — and a Pi that browns out during an SD-card write can lose its filesystem. Keeping motor power and logic power on distinct regulated rails, joined only by a common ground, is standard practice on essentially every motor-driven robot, and it echoes the same power-distribution reasoning Lecture 11 walked through for LIMO's own battery and BMS.

✅ Forge now has power distribution. Flip the module's switch and it delivers 12 V to the motors and a clean 5 V rail that, from §12.11 onward, will run the robot's brain.

12.11The brain — Ubuntu Server, static IP & SSH

Forge's brain is a Raspberry Pi 4 running Ubuntu Server 22.04 LTS (64-bit) — a headless, no-desktop build, because a robot you drive around a room is not a machine you want to plug a monitor into. Ubuntu (rather than Raspberry Pi OS) because ROS 2 Humble targets Ubuntu 22.04 directly.

💡 Two ways in — pick SSH

You can set the Pi up either over SSH (a network protocol for secure remote access — you work from your own PC) or with a screen and keyboard (mini-HDMI plus USB). SSH is dramatically more convenient once you're chasing a robot around a room, and the rest of this course assumes it. The screen-and-keyboard route stays available as a recovery path if networking goes wrong.

Flashing the card

Use the Raspberry Pi Imager. On Ubuntu:

$ sudo apt update $ sudo apt install snapd $ sudo snap install rpi-imager

On Windows or macOS, download it from raspberrypi.com/software.

  1. Insert the microSD card into your PC (use a USB-SD adapter if needed) and open Imager.
  2. Choose Raspberry Pi 4 as the device.
  3. Choose Other general-purpose OS → Ubuntu → Ubuntu Server 22.04 LTS (64-bit) as the operating system.
  4. Choose your SD card as the storage. Check the capacity to be sure you have the right device — this step is destructive.
  5. Click Next. When the Use OS customisation? popup appears, choose EDIT SETTINGS.
  6. On the GENERAL tab: set hostname and username both to fastbot, set a password, tick Configure wireless LAN and enter your SSID and Wi-Fi password, and tick Set locale settings for your region and keyboard layout.
  7. On the SERVICES tab: tick Enable SSH. Do not skip this.
  8. SAVE, answer YES to the customisation popup, and flash the card.
⚠️ Forgetting "Enable SSH" is the classic own-goal

A headless install with SSH disabled is only reachable by plugging in a monitor and keyboard — which defeats the entire point, and on a Pi buried inside a chassis means partial disassembly. Double-check that checkbox before writing the image, every single time.

Setting a static IP before first boot

When a new device joins your network, your router's DHCP server hands it a more-or-less random address. That's fine for a laptop and miserable for a robot: you need the IP to SSH in, and you're about to hardcode connection details into scripts and launch files. Fix it before the Pi ever boots.

  1. With the freshly flashed card connected to your PC, find the system-boot volume that appears.
  2. Open the file /system-boot/network-config (you'll need permission to modify it).
  3. Find the wifis section and replace it with the block below.
/system-boot/network-config
network:
  version: 2
  renderer: networkd
  wifis:
    wlan0:
      dhcp4: no
      optional: true            # allow booting even if Wi-Fi is unavailable
      addresses:
        - 192.168.1.100/24      # the static IP you are claiming
      gateway4: 192.168.1.1     # your router's IP
      nameservers:
        addresses:
          - 8.8.8.8
          - 1.1.1.1
      access-points:
        "SSID_WIFI_NETWORK_HERE":     # double-check this is exactly right
          password: "PASSWORD_HERE"   # and this
🔩 Adjust these to your network
  • dhcp4: no stops the router assigning a random address.
  • addresses: - 192.168.1.100/24 claims 192.168.1.100. Pick something outside your router's DHCP pool so nothing else is ever handed the same address.
  • gateway4 must be your actual router address — commonly 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1.
  • The SSID and password are typed here in plain text and are unforgiving about typos. A wrong character means a Pi that boots and never appears on the network.

Save, eject the card, insert it into the Pi, and power it on. After a minute or two:

$ ssh fastbot@192.168.1.100 fastbot@192.168.1.100's password: Welcome to Ubuntu 22.04.5 LTS (GNU/Linux 5.15.0-1053-raspi aarch64) ... fastbot@fastbot:~$

✅ You are inside Forge's brain, over the network, with no monitor or keyboard ever attached to it.

12.12Installing ROS 2 Humble (and fixing a real bug)

Ubuntu Server ships with nothing ROS-related. Forge's Pi needs the same ROS 2 Humble install every other machine in this course has used — except this time you run it yourself, over SSH, on a completely blank system. Every command below runs on the Pi.

1 — Update, and set the locale

$ sudo apt update && sudo apt upgrade -y $ locale # check for UTF-8 $ sudo apt update && sudo apt install locales $ sudo locale-gen en_US en_US.UTF-8 $ sudo update-locale LC_ALL=en_US.UTF-8 LANG=en_US.UTF-8 $ export LANG=en_US.UTF-8 $ locale # verify

2 — Enable the required repositories and add the ROS 2 key

$ sudo apt install software-properties-common $ sudo add-apt-repository universe $ sudo apt update && sudo apt install curl -y $ sudo curl -sSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ros/rosdistro/master/ros.key \ -o /usr/share/keyrings/ros-archive-keyring.gpg

3 — Add the repository to your sources list

$ echo "deb [arch=$(dpkg --print-architecture) \ signed-by=/usr/share/keyrings/ros-archive-keyring.gpg] http://packages.ros.org/ros2/ubuntu \ $(. /etc/os-release && echo $UBUNTU_CODENAME) main" | \ sudo tee /etc/apt/sources.list.d/ros2.list > /dev/null

4 — Install development tools and ROS 2

$ sudo apt update && sudo apt install ros-dev-tools git-all -y $ sudo apt update && sudo apt upgrade -y $ sudo apt install ros-humble-ros-base ros-humble-demo-nodes-cpp \ ros-humble-teleop-twist-keyboard -y
💡 Why ros-base and not desktop

ros-humble-ros-base is the bare communication stack plus core tooling — no RViz, no visualization, nothing that wants a display. That's exactly right for a headless robot: it installs faster, uses far less of the SD card, and doesn't drag in GUI dependencies the Pi will never use. RViz runs on your machine, subscribing over the network. teleop_twist_keyboard comes along because Lecture 13 uses it to drive Forge by hand.

5 — Source ROS 2 in every new shell

ROS 2 has to be sourced in each new shell, which is normally handled by adding one line to ~/.bashrc:

$ nano ~/.bashrc
⚠️ Spot the bug

You just installed ros-humble-* packages. A student adds this line to the bottom of ~/.bashrc: 

### rest of .bashrc

source /opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash

They save, open a new terminal, and run ros2 topic list — and nothing works, or the shell complains that the file doesn't exist. What's wrong? Work it out before reading the next box. (This exact mistake appears in the published reference material for this build, which is precisely why it's worth practising: copy-pasted setup lines pointing at the wrong distribution are one of the most common real-world ROS 2 environment bugs, on any robot.)

Exercise · Intermediate — diagnose it yourself

Work through the diagnosis rather than jumping to the fix:

  • What command lists which ROS distributions are actually installed? (Hint: ls /opt/ros/.)
  • What does echo $ROS_DISTRO print in a shell where the wrong line was sourced, versus a correct one?
  • What error does source itself give when the path doesn't exist — and why is it easy to miss in a login banner?

Then fix the line — it should read source /opt/ros/humble/setup.bash — re-source your shell, and confirm with ros2 doctor or ros2 topic list.

~/.bashrc (corrected)
### rest of .bashrc

source /opt/ros/humble/setup.bash

6 — Verify the installation end to end

Two SSH sessions into the Pi, running the classic talker/listener pair:

$ source ~/.bashrc # --- SSH session 1 --- $ ros2 run demo_nodes_cpp talker [INFO] [talker]: Publishing: 'Hello World: 1' [INFO] [talker]: Publishing: 'Hello World: 2' # --- SSH session 2, same Pi --- $ ros2 run demo_nodes_cpp listener [INFO] [listener]: I heard: [Hello World: 1] [INFO] [listener]: I heard: [Hello World: 2]

Two independent processes publishing and subscribing over ROS 2, on hardware you flashed, networked and configured yourself. ✅ Forge's brain is alive.

7 — Move the Pi onto the robot's own power

Last step of the session: unplug the Pi from its mains charger and run it from the power module's regulated 5 V rail, so the whole robot lives off one battery.

⚠️ Watch the Pi's undervoltage warning

Reboot and SSH back in, then check dmesg | grep -i voltage and look for the Pi's low-voltage lightning-bolt warnings. The Pi 4 is genuinely fussy about a clean 5 V under load, and an undervolted Pi behaves like a flaky Pi — random freezes, dropped USB devices, corrupted SD cards — while reporting nothing obviously wrong. This is the same "no error is not the same as working" discipline Lecture 11 built around LIMO's error_code field, and Lecture 13 turns it into a full debugging exercise.

🧠 Quick Quiz — Session 12

Q1. Forge's BOM lists a rear ball caster. What breaks if you leave it out?

Reveal answer

A two-wheel differential-drive robot has only two ground contacts, which isn't statically stable — without a third contact point the chassis tips forward or backward under its own weight or any acceleration.

Q2. Why does the encoder need its own 3.3-5 V power pin, separate from the motor terminals?

Reveal answer

The encoder is a low-power digital signal source, while the motor terminals carry high-current drive power at up to 12 V. Feeding the encoder from the motor supply — or from any rail outside its rated range — risks destroying it.

Q3. Why does the motor loop run on an Arduino Nano instead of the Raspberry Pi?

Reveal answer

Three reasons: dedicated hardware PWM timers give a rock-steady signal; a dedicated MCU gives real-time, predictable response with no OS scheduling jitter; and it frees the Pi for ROS 2, perception and navigation. It's the same SBC/embedded-controller split LIMO uses (Lecture 11).

Q4. On Forge, ENA and ENB are jumpered to 5 V. So where does speed control come from?

Reveal answer

From PWM on the IN pins. D5, D6, D9 and D10 are all hardware-PWM-capable on the ATmega328P, and ros_arduino_bridge drives them with a duty cycle while holding the opposite input low. Enable stays permanently high.

Q5. Your encoder measures 3450 counts/rev and the PID loop runs at 30 Hz. What do you send to spin at 1 rev/s, and why that number?

Reveal answer

m 115 115. 3450 counts/rev ÷ 30 loops/sec = 115 counts accumulated per PID loop cycle when the wheel turns once per second.

Q6. A pack has plenty of capacity (Ah) but too low a C-rating for Forge's peak current. What symptom would you expect under load?

Reveal answer

Voltage sag under load, or the pack's own protection circuitry tripping — the same warning sign Lecture 11 taught you to watch for in LIMO's battery_voltage field.

Q7. (Gotcha) You installed ros-humble-* packages, sourced .bashrc, and ros2 topic list still misbehaves. Where's the bug most likely hiding?

Reveal answer

In the distribution name inside the sourced path. A very common copy-paste mistake is source /opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash when Humble is what's installed — it should read source /opt/ros/humble/setup.bash. Confirm with ls /opt/ros/ and echo $ROS_DISTRO.

Glossary

H-bridge
A switch arrangement that lets a motor be driven in either direction by choosing which diagonal pair is closed. Forge's is an L298N; LIMO's was sealed inside the chassis (Lecture 11).
PWM (Pulse-Width Modulation)
Controlling delivered power by switching a signal on and off rapidly and varying the fraction of time it's on (the duty cycle).
Duty cycle
The on-fraction of a PWM signal. 100% = full power, 50% ≈ half power, 0% = off.
Deadband
The low range of duty cycles where a motor buzzes but doesn't turn, because the commanded power can't overcome static friction.
Open loop / closed loop
Open loop commands power and hopes. Closed loop reads back actual motion (from encoders) and corrects the command until reality matches the request.
Quadrature encoder
Two phase-offset output channels (A and B) whose edge ordering reveals both amount and direction of rotation.
Counts per revolution (CPR)
Encoder counts accumulated in one full wheel revolution. A property of your specific motor and gearbox — measure it, don't assume it.
Power budget
A component-by-component tally of V×I for every part, summed to a worst-case total draw and used to size the battery.
C-rating
A pack's maximum safe continuous discharge current, as a multiple of its capacity: Capacity(Ah) × C.
Voltage sag
The drop in pack voltage under heavy load; severe sag indicates the C-rating is too low for the peak current being drawn.
Netplan
Ubuntu's YAML network configuration system, used here (via network-config on the boot partition) to claim a static IP before first boot.
Headless
A computer run without a directly attached monitor or keyboard, administered over the network via SSH.

Common mistakes

  • Wiring a motor by remembered wire colour instead of checking that specific unit's published pinout.
  • Connecting the battery with reversed polarity — the one failure in §12.5 that costs hardware rather than 30 seconds.
  • Powering MCU or Pi logic from the same unregulated rail as the motors, exposing them to switching spikes.
  • Using a firmware baud rate that doesn't match the serial tool: no error, just garbage bytes.
  • Trusting a hardcoded encoder_cpr default (2500 or 2550 in the reference launch files) instead of measuring your own.
  • Sizing a battery for capacity alone while ignoring its C-rating against actual peak current.
  • Forgetting to tick Enable SSH during flashing, then needing a monitor and keyboard to recover a headless board.
  • Sourcing the wrong ROS distribution's setup.bash in .bashrc.
  • Running the Pi from a rail that browns out under motor load, then blaming ROS 2 for the resulting flakiness.
